# Textbook

Use for mathematics or technical teaching material. Introduce a problem,
motivation, or familiar object before its formal definition, theorem, notation,
or example. Weave exposition through formal blocks:

`narrative → formal statement → explanation/example → consequence`.

**Bad:** a section opens directly with a definition box before the reader knows
why the object exists.

**Good:** two or three sentences motivate the object and state the section's
goal, then the definition box, then exposition that unpacks its conditions and
connects them to what follows.

State domains, assumptions, and symbol meanings before formulas. A proof is a
continuous argument: establish the starting facts, explain each non-obvious
transition, and state the resulting claim. Do not replace proof logic with an
unexplained list of “steps”.

**Bad:** display the identity first and append “for $X \in \mathfrak{r}$ and
$Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$” afterward.

**Good:** “取任意 $X \in \mathfrak{r}$ 与 $Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$” precedes the
display.

**Bad:** “Step 1: … Step 2: … Step 3: …” as labeled fragments whose content does
not connect.

**Good:** continuous prose in which each transition states what it uses and what
it yields — “由于 $U \in SU(4)$ 且指数映射满，存在 $X \in \mathfrak{su}(4)$ 使
$\exp(X) = U$。接下来把 $X$ 按 Hamiltonian 基展开……定理得证。”

Use examples to make an abstract definition operational.
Keep a consistent theorem, definition, example, remark, and exercise hierarchy.
Do not add generic process commentary or ornamental emphasis.
